A \glossary{large language model} (LLM) based on the
\glossary{transformer architecture} typically generates text responses to text inputs by
repeatedly predicting the next word or token in a sequence.\footnote{The
concept of token is introduced in chapter \extref{ch_word_embeddings}.
Following practice introduced there we will vacillate between ``token'', which
is more accurate (since it encompasses punctuation, word parts, and other
non-word entities) and ``word'', which is more intuitive.} They are trained on
large datasets of everyday text, like text from the internet, which is easily
available. As noted in section \extref{age_generative_ai}, it is common to
equate ``transformer'' with ``LLM'', but the two concepts are distinct. The
transformer is a neural network architecture, while an LLM is any model
of language generation trained on a large dataset.
In this chapter we focus on transformer-based models of text
generation like GPT.\footnote{An LLM can be built
out of something besides transformers, and transformers can process non-linguistic data. For example, by the mid-2020s some state-of-the-art image classification
models were transformer based, and OpenAI's video
generation model Sora (2024) ran on a transformer architecture. Transformer-based LLMs include
OpenAI's GPT series, Google's Gemini, Anthropic's Claude, and Meta's Llama.
Most of these models can only be accessed online but some can be
downloaded and run locally, further fine-tuned, etc. The Simbrain simulation
\simref{Language $>$ (Beta) Pretrained language model (LFM2.5)} runs one such model locally. A list of LLMs ranked by
how well they chat is here: \weburl{https://lmarena.ai/leaderboard}.} We will
sometimes refer simply to ``LLMs'', by which we mean transformer-based
LLMs.\footnote{There are numerous high-quality online resources for learning
about LLMs. An excellent visual introduction is at this website:
\weburl{https://poloclub.github.io/transformer-explainer/}. 3Blue1Brown is always
excellent on visual intuition and he has a YouTube video:
\weburl{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjZofJX0v4M}. For a more technical
walkthrough on building an LLM from scratch see Karpathy's tutorial:
\weburl{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kCc8FmEb1nY}.}

Earlier efforts at text generation and natural language processing used
supervised recurrent networks (chapter \extref{ch_supervised_recurrent}), which
are limited in various ways, as we saw. They can only process a
small amount of context, and suffer from the vanishing gradient problem. The
transformer architecture addresses these issues using a feed-forward network that can
be ``aware'' of multiple kinds of relationships between arbitrarily far-flung
parts of an input stream. Because it is a feed-forward network, the many insights of earlier research 
can be applied. In
particular, all the lessons of the deep learning revolution (section
\extref{deep_revolution}) apply here, and indeed, transformers are many-layered
deep networks (chapter \extref{ch_cnn}) that make good use of both
\glossary{representational width} and \glossary{representational depth}. They
can be trained on large datasets using highly optimized parallel hardware. Like
all the other networks discussed in this book, they are not just useful as
engineered tools, but are highly relevant both to neuroscience and cognitive
science, and seem to develop meaningful internal representations. 

The inner workings of a transformer are complex, so we begin by treating the
network as a black box. We discuss how transformer-based LLMs are trained using
widely available text data, how a special recursive trick can be used to make a
feed-forward network---which only predicts next words---produce meaningful
conversations, and how those conversations unfold in a context window that acts
like a working memory. After considering the network's outputs and
hyperparameters, we open the box and discuss how the transformer architecture
itself works. The relevance of these models to cognitive science, neuroscience,
and philosophy is the topic of chapter \extref{ch_llm_cogsci}, and how LLMs are
used in practice (post-training, prompting, and agentic systems) is the topic of
chapter \extref{ch_llms_practice}.

Changes in this area are rapid, and the relevance of these areas to cognitive
science only began to be studied in the early 2020s, so frequent updates to these chapters are expected.

\section{Learning to Speak Internetese}\label{internetese}

% Another approach to describing this. There is a kind of I (JKY) sometimes have, like what if I hundreds of years to study everything. Well, this is kind of what such a person would be like.  Except they did in a month with a very large brain and an insane force-feeding regimen.

LLMs are trained on an enormous amount of text. The training set for GPT-3 was
a large subset of the \emph{entire internet}, which included all of Wikipedia,
several compilations of books, and a web-scraped archive of the internet called
``Common Crawl'' (\weburl{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Crawl}). See
figure \ref{gptDatasets}. Similar datasets are used to train all frontier LLMs (the most advanced
large-scale models, such as Claude and ChatGPT).
If you've ever written anything online, there is a decent chance it is part of
the training data of one of those models.\footnote{Language models have a
longer history. In section \extref{languageModelsRecurrent} we saw how
recurrent networks trained on example text learn to produce text with the
statistical properties of that text: a network trained on Shakespeare produces
fake Shakespeare, and one trained on math papers produces fake math.
Transformers do the same thing much better, partly because the architecture is
better suited to the task, and partly because they can be efficiently trained on
far larger datasets (hence the ``large'' in ``large language model'').}

The internet contains discussions of every topic of human endeavor, from physics
to history, along with plenty of gossip and popular culture. Since LLMs are
trained on all of this, they can talk about all of it, statistically
generalizing from training data that encompasses much of recorded human
knowledge. In a sense, they learn to speak ``internetese''.

But internetese is a strange language. In fact a raw model trained in this way, what is also known as a
\glossary{base model}, does not reliably answer questions or hold a conversation. It
continues text the way that text might continue on the internet. Give it a
question and it might reply like a Redditor, then shift into the tone of a
Wikipedia article, then invent a comment thread or a website footer full of
links to pages that don't exist. Its tone and persona keep changing, because
it is not one speaker but a statistical model of millions of them. This is what
raw internetese sounds like. 

Base models only become the butler-like chatbots we are familiar with after a further process of \glossary{fine-tuning} (part of what is also called \glossary{post-training}), which turns a base model into a \glossary{chat model} that answers
questions, follows instructions, and knows when to stop talking. This process involves further refinement of weights using special techniques, and targeted human feedback. It's a kind of finishing school for wild and crazy base models. This step is
essential to producing a usable conversational
partner. Fine-tuning is discussed in chapter \extref{ch_llms_practice}.

\section{Training Using Next-Word Prediction}

Recall from chapter \extref{ch_data_science} that a labeled dataset consists of
inputs and targets. As noted there, this kind of labeled data can be hard to
obtain. We might have lots of pictures of people but not know their names or
identities, or have lots of pictures of cats and dogs but not have labeled
whether a given picture is of a cat or a dog. Creating those target labels by
hand for a large dataset is labor-intensive, as it requires humans to manually
look over each picture. Next-token prediction gets around this problem of needing labeled target data, because the 
targets are endogenous to the training data itself.\footnote{This insight is not new with language models. Neural network models of next-word prediction have been used at least as far back as \cite{elman1990finding}. Again see chapter \extref{ch_supervised_recurrent}. }

% What is actually done is that multiple training examples are created from a given piece of text. We slide this window across the entire sequence so that each token predicts the one that follows it.
Each sequence is used to predict the next item in that sequence.
LLMs like GPT take \emph{any passage of text} and convert it into a labeled
dataset that can be used to train a neural network, by predicting each next
token from the tokens before it (\glossary{auto-regression}). The trick is, roughly, to treat the vector
embeddings (chapter \extref{ch_word_embeddings}) of a sequence of text tokens (minus the last token in the sequence)
as one big input, and then to treat the final token in
the sequence, encoded as a one-hot vector over all possible tokens, as the target.
We are still using supervised learning, as in chapter \extref{ch_supervised},
associating inputs with targets or labels, but the targets are not provided
separately and are instead right there in the training data. It's extremely
convenient!  This is called \glossary{self-supervised learning}.\footnote{Self-supervised learning
is any form of learning where targets can be created using only the input signal. Auto-regression means predicting each item in a sequence from the items that came before it. The two concepts are closely related but distinct. We can have auto-regression without self-supervision: an image captioning model generates captions autoregressively, one word at a time, but is trained on captions written by people, so its training is supervised in the ordinary sense. We can also have self-supervision without auto-regression: a BERT-style model hides a word in the middle of a sentence and predicts it using the words after it as well as the words before it, so it is not predicting each word from earlier words only. LLMs like GPT are both self-supervised and autoregressive.}
Self-supervised training can be used to generate a labeled dataset
from any text document. We no longer have the difficulty of creating
``ground-truth'' labels, as the term ``supervised learning'' may imply. In
practice it's much more like an unsupervised algorithm, in that all we need in
order to train the model is a bunch of text.

For example, consider this block of text adapted from the Wikipedia page for UC
Merced:

\begin{quote}
The University of California, Merced is a public land-grant research university
in Merced, California. It is one of the ten campuses in the University of
California (UC) system. Established in 2005, Merced is the newest campus within
the UC system.
\end{quote}

From this excerpt, we create a bunch of training examples, a list of
input/target pairings. For example, ``The University of'' is used to predict ``California'', and ``The University of California, Merced is a public'' is used to predict ``land''.\footnote{In training, this is just one long sequence where the target
sequence is a version of the input shifted one token to the left.}

The network itself does not directly process text tokens; they must first be converted into a numerical format. Each token in the input is turned into a vector using a word or token embedding (the two terms are synonymous; see chapter \extref{ch_word_embeddings}). The target is also
a vector, but it is not an embedding. Instead it is a
one-hot vector over all possible tokens, where all entries in the
vector are 0 except for the actual next token's entry, which is 1. (We discuss
this further in section \ref{llmOutput}.) In this way text data is converted into input-target vector pairs, which we can use to train a feed-forward neural network (see figure
\ref{nextWordPrediction}).\footnote{Note that a longer word like ``university''
would normally be broken up into multiple tokens: \texttt{un} and
\texttt{iversity} is a typical tokenization for it. We are neglecting this
complication here to keep things simple. More on tokenization in chapter
\extref{ch_word_embeddings}.}  

So, the input to a network is generated by taking all the tokens in the input and producing a vector for each one using the embedding. We will think of this in terms of a matrix, as in figure \ref{nextWordPrediction}, where each row corresponds
to the token embedding for one token. This input has the \glossary{shape} (sequence size,
embedding dimension). It is a direct numerical representation of the input text. It is useful to think of it as a stack of vectors, one for each token in context. The network then transforms these stacks of vectors.\footnote{What is actually fed to the transformer is a batch of these
token embedding matrices, that is, a rank-3 tensor with shape (batch size,
sequence size, embedding dimension; see section \extref{sect_tensors}).}

The word embeddings in figure \ref{nextWordPrediction} and figure
\ref{sequenceDim} are 4-dimensional (each token is associated with an array of
4 numbers, a vector in a 4d space). In practice, this ``embedding dimension''
is quite a bit larger. For example, the embedding dimension is over 12,000 for GPT-3 (see figure \ref{gptParams}).\footnote{Bigger is not always better here. Since the embedding dimension affects the size of most weight matrices in the network, some very large models kept it modest and put most of their parameters elsewhere. For example, DeepSeek-V3 (2024) had 671 billion parameters, far more than GPT-3, but an embedding dimension of about 7,000.}

Each token in the stack of token embeddings sent to the network is processed separately through its many layers, acquiring different meanings and context as it does.\footnote{Recall the discussion of matrix multiplication and the ``row processing'' perspective (section \extref{matrixMultiplication}), where we can view a matrix multiplication as taking a stack of $N$ row vectors on the left, multiplying them by a matrix on the right, to produce another stack of $N$ row vectors, each of which has been \emph{separately processed} by the matrix on the right.  These ideas apply here.  In fact, with the exception of the self-attention matrix (which considers all the relationships between tokens), all other processing in an LLM operates separately on each token's representation. As a result, we can separately track the transformations a token's vector representation goes through as it makes its way through a network.  We will return to this idea below when we discuss the ``residual stream''.} For example, consider the word ``you'' in figure \ref{sequenceDim}. This is the same word repeated twice, so at the input level, it is associated with the same vector embedding, in this case $(1,2,5,6)$.\footnote{This is not quite right, since something called a positional encoding is often added even at the input level. But even when these positional encodings are removed the points made here apply.} But as the two tokens are processed through the network, their representation will change, reflecting their different positions and functions in the sentence.

\section{The Recursive Trick}\label{recursiveTrick}

One confusing thing about figure \ref{nextWordPrediction} is that we go from a
large input covering a whole sequence of tokens to a single token as output. So
great, we can predict single words. But how do we go from single words to
generating long text outputs, or having conversations? In fact, in hearing
about generative AI as ``next-word prediction'' machines, you may have
sometimes wondered how such complicated things can happen when all these models
do is predict next words.

% More discussion of special tokens like end-of-sequence is probably needed,
% and maybe further up. The network itself is feedforward, but its interaction
% with an environment is recursive.
The answer is what we can call the ``recursive trick'': take the network's
output, append it to the input, and feed the result back in. Then repeat.
That's it! We feed the network a sequence of tokens, and it predicts the next
token. That token is appended to the input, and this longer input is fed back
to the network, which predicts another token, and so on. This technique can be
used to generate unending sequences of text from any prompt. The prompt is our input, and
then answers are generated using the recursive trick. Text will continue to be
generated until a special end-of-sequence token is reached (in a chat, an
end-of-turn token).
%\footnote{For more on the post-training that enables ChatGPT and other frontier models to carry on a conversation with a human-like persona, see \extref{ch_ai_alignment}.}
% Also RLAIF — Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback

Suppose, for example, we want to ask a network ``hello how are you?'' The input
to the network is the whole sequence (``hello'', ``how'', ``are'', ``you'',
``?''). Let's  not worry about the vector embeddings, and just see the
general idea, as shown in figure \ref{gptRecursedInputs}. Notice that the
initial prompt is the initial input, but then the prompt \emph{and} the first
word of the response are used as the next input, and this process can be
repeated until a response is written out.
  
\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\accessibleimage[scale=.55]{./images/gptRecursedInputs.png}{Autoregressive GPT process. A context window of tokens and embeddings enters stacked transformer blocks to predict a next token; the predicted token is added to the context and the process repeats.}
\caption[Jeff Yoshimi.]{A schematic view of how ``conversations'' are generated
from feed-forward networks in systems like ChatGPT. The output at one time is
concatenated to the input at that time, and that is then used as the input at
the next time step. This process is repeated to generate a full response.
}
\label{gptRecursedInputs}
\end{figure}

So this gets us one response. But then you type a new question. That
\emph{entire question} is appended to the \emph{entire past conversation}
including both what you and the model have said so far. This growing record of
the conversation is the model's \glossary{context window}, or more concisely,
its ``context''.\footnote{Usage has not stabilized yet, and these two terms
(``context'' and ``context window'') are often used interchangeably. However,
sometimes ``context window'' is used to emphasize the \emph{size} (in number of
tokens) of the context.} Everything the model processes ``in context'' is this
one sequence of tokens (figure \ref{chatContext}).

\label{chatContext}
\end{figure}

How does the model know when to stop and let you talk? The context window
contains more than the words you see. It also contains \emph{special tokens},
which are usually hidden in a chat interface. Special tokens mark the start of
a document, the start and end of each turn, who is speaking (the user, the
model, or the system), and other structure such as requests to use tools. When
the model generates an end-of-turn token, the software running the model (often
called a \emph{harness}) stops generating, displays the response, and waits for
your next message. It then wraps your message in the appropriate special tokens,
appends it to the context, and starts the recursive trick again. Chat models
learn to use these tokens during fine-tuning (section \ref{internetese}), which
trains them on conversations formatted with special tokens. Special tokens and
harnesses are discussed further in section \extref{specialTokens}.\footnote{In
the Simbrain simulation
\simref{Language $>$ (Beta) Pretrained language model (LFM2.5)}, all tokens in the context window are visible, including special
tokens like \texttt{<|im\_start|>} and \texttt{<|im\_end|>}, which open and
close each turn.}

Of course, as we keep doing this, the inputs to the network get larger and
larger. There must be some limit to how far we can go, right? The answer is
yes. An LLM's hyperparameters include a context window size: the
maximum number of tokens the LLM will read. As we will see in section
\ref{inContext}, this is something like the capacity of a working memory. At the start of a
chat, the context is small, containing only an initial prompt. It grows as the
prompts from a person and the responses from the LLM are added.\footnote{Figures
\ref{nextWordPrediction} and \ref{sequenceDim} fill out the input with zeros
or blank rows, as if the network always took an input of a fixed size. This
kind of zero \emph{padding} is sometimes used in training, so that sequences of
different lengths can be grouped into batches, with the padded positions masked
out so they have no effect. But when a single conversation is processed there is no padding.
The input matrix has one row for each token in the context so far, and it grows
by one row with each new token. The Simbrain simulation
\simref{Language $>$ Tiny language model} does use zero padding, which makes
the growth easy to see: its matrices have a fixed number of rows, and they fill
up one row at a time as text is generated.} So long as the conversation fits, all the
prompts and responses up to the current point are included as part of the
input, and the LLM uses the recursive trick to generate new responses that are
sensitive to everything that's been discussed thus far. When the conversation
grows larger than the context window, something has to give. The simplest
approach is to drop text from the start of the context (from a computer science
standpoint, this is a queue). Many systems instead
\emph{compact} the context, replacing older parts of the conversation with a
summary, or store information outside the context and retrieve it when
needed.

Context windows can be remarkably large. GPT-3 has a context window of about 2,000 tokens or about 4 pages of text, and early versions of GPT-4 had context windows of 32,000 tokens or about 64 pages of text. By the mid-2020s, some models had context windows of a million tokens or more.

\section{In-Context Processing}\label{inContext}

Thus far in the book we have focused on learning via incremental updates to the
weights and biases of a neural network. LLMs are no exception; they learn via updates to their billions or trillions of
parameters. However, LLMs have a remarkable feature: an ability to process a great deal of information at inference time, in their context window. Recall from the introduction (section
\extref{performanceLearning}) that inference (or performance) means using
learned parameters to process inputs, as opposed to updating those parameters
during training. In-context processing happens entirely at inference time. This is a bit like a working memory system, which processes information and can come up with novel insights without making any updates to weights. This is the basis for much of the power of LLMs. Without any weight training, they can reason their way through a problem or innovate a new idea.

This is often called \glossary{in-context learning}, though perhaps
``in-context processing'' is a better description. The word ``learning'' here
is \emph{not} a reference to training weights, but rather to how an LLM can do
new things by combining its prior training with information in the context
window. It learns in the same way a person starting a new job can learn how to
do something using instructions held in working memory together with their
background knowledge. A classic example is \glossary{few-shot} learning, where
an LLM performs a novel task based on just a few examples given in the context
window. For example, an LLM can be given examples of a new type of character in
a game and asked to classify some new ones. It can do this even though none of
its weights or biases have been updated.\footnote{When only a single example is
given, this is called \glossary{one-shot learning}. When no examples are given
and the model performs a task based on instructions alone, this is called
\glossary{zero-shot} learning. For example, an LLM can be given a review like
``This cake was delicious'' and asked if it is positive or negative, and
correctly answer that it's positive. Most everyday use of chat models is
zero-shot.} This marked an important advance in the
capabilities of neural networks.\footnote{This addresses an old critique of neural
networks, going back to the 1980s: that while humans can learn things in a
single trial, neural networks could not \cite{schneider1988structure}.
Transformers show that neural networks can do this, albeit in networks quite
different from the brain. How psychologically or neurally realistic this form
of learning is had only begun to be explored by the mid-2020s (chapter
\extref{ch_llm_cogsci}). For some discussion of the context window as a kind of
working memory, see \cite{yoshimi2026phenomenology}.} Indeed, the paper introducing
GPT-3 was titled ``Language Models are Few-Shot Learners'' \cite{brown2020language}.

Although AI researchers call this a form of ``learning'', from the standpoint of cognitive science it's more like processing information in working memory. But if we take this analogy seriously, then LLMs are like systems with vastly inhuman working memory.  Human working memory holds only a handful of items at once (the
classic 7 $\pm$ 2). By the mid-2020s, frontier LLMs could hold hundreds or thousands of pages in their context. That's like a person being able to hold a whole book in memory in the same way they can hold a phone number in working memory.\footnote{How much
of its context a model can actively make use of at once is a separate
question. Relevant here is the discovery that merely giving an LLM a larger
context window does not automatically give it a correspondingly large working
memory. Though a model may hold an entire book in its context,
their attention is spread thin over such a large context and their ability to
successfully cite information will not work as well as it does in a more
reasonably sized context \cite{liu2024lost}.}

\section{Softmax Outputs}\label{llmOutput}

The first step in a transformer is to embed inputs, converting all the tokens in a conversation into a matrix of inputs, as we've seen. The next step is to transform these inputs using a series of transformer blocks, which are discussed in section \ref{transformers}. When all the processing is done, the final step is to convert vectors back to predicted next tokens. Thus the final
 layer does the opposite of what the embedding layer does. Rather than converting from tokens to activations, it converts from activations to tokens, and is thus a ``de-embedding'' layer.

% Cohere with simbrain
The output of an LLM is a \glossary{softmax} layer (section
\extref{wta_softmax_section}) with one output for each token in the vocabulary.
In the example of figure \ref{nextWordPrediction} the vocabulary contains just 7
tokens, and the targets used in training are one-hot vectors over these 7 tokens.
(Later figures add an 8th token, the period.) But the outputs the network actually produces are
probability distributions over them (figure \ref{transformerOverview}). They say
how likely each token is to come next, given all the prompts and responses in
the context window so far, and given the patterns in the training data. The
error on the training data can never go to zero, because the same context is
followed by different tokens in different places in the training data. The best
the network can do is learn how probable each token is in a given context, and
that is exactly what we want.\footnote{Since the targets are one-hot encodings
(section \extref{wrangling}), the task given the network is technically a
classification task (section \extref{classificationRegression}), classifying
input texts according to what token is likely to occur next. However,
classification here serves as a \emph{proxy task}. By \emph{trying} to
perfectly classify next tokens, the network ends up producing good
probabilities, which is what we are actually after.}

\centering
\accessibleimage[scale=.2]{./images/TransformerOverview.png}{Transformer overview: a context window passes through an embedding layer and stacked transformer blocks to a softmax output. Each block contains multiple attention heads followed by an MLP.} \; \; \;
\accessibleimage[scale=.2]{./images/TransformerOverview2.png}{Transformer overview with a GPT model-size table. The network maps a context window through embeddings, repeated transformer blocks, and softmax output; the table lists GPT-3 variants with parameter counts and training settings.}

}
\label{transformerOverview}
\end{figure}

Once we have a probability distribution over tokens, one of the most probable
tokens is randomly selected, and that becomes the output.\footnote{There are
several ways to do this. A greedy method always picks the most probable token,
but this can lead to overly predictable outputs. Top-$k$ sampling randomly
samples from the $k$ most probable tokens. Top-$p$ sampling samples from
however many of the most probable tokens it takes for their probabilities to
add up to a given value $p$, often somewhere in the range of 0.8 to 0.95.}

The softmax temperature parameter can be used here (see section
\extref{wta_softmax_section}): higher temperatures will add more probability to less likely tokens, making the output more
random or ``creative''.  Lower temperatures produce more sharply peaked probability distributions, making the output more predictable.\footnote{Temperature and sampling strategies can be explored in the Simbrain simulation \simref{Language $>$ Tiny language model}.}

\section{Parameters and Hyperparameters}

To get a more concrete sense of the scale and structure of an LLM, we can
consider its \glossary{hyperparameters}. Recall that hyperparameters are values
that determine the structure of a network or how it is trained, but that are
not themselves updated during training. Figure \ref{gptParams} shows several of
them for different versions of GPT-3, including the number of layers
($n_\text{layers}$), corresponding to representational depth, the number of
heads ($n_\text{heads}$), corresponding to representational width, the size of
the token embedding ($d_\text{model}$), and the learning rate and batch size.
The size of the context window (section \ref{recursiveTrick}) is another
hyperparameter, though it is not shown in the figure. Notice that these are all concepts we have seen in one way or another (often
directly) in earlier chapters. This is remarkable. The same ideas used to train
tiny networks, where we can see every weight and every activation, are being
used here on a massively larger scale.

Just how much larger is shown in the column labeled $n_\text{params}$, the
number of parameters, that is, the total number of weights and biases, which
\emph{are} updated during training. This number is not set directly. It follows
from hyperparameters like the number of layers and the size of the token
embedding. The released version of GPT-3 had 175 billion parameters. Compare
the XOR network of section \extref{sect_xor_remap}, with 9 parameters, or the
three-object detector of chapter \extref{ch_intro}, with about 50. A standard
convolutional network might have millions. Later networks were even larger:
GPT-4 (2023) reportedly had about 1.76 trillion parameters.\footnote{It takes large server banks that consume huge
amounts of energy to run these models, especially when training them. There is an active thread of research
attempting to achieve similar performance with smaller models, some of which
can be run on a  personal computer. For example, Gemma 2B achieves performance
similar to GPT-3.5 on some benchmarks with only 2 billion parameters. See
\weburl{https://developers.googleblog.com/en/smaller-safer-more-transparent-advancing-responsible-ai-with-gemma/}.}

\section{The Transformer Architecture}\label{transformers}

We have thus far covered high-level features of how LLMs work, but have treated
the transformer as a black box. The time has come to open up the box. How does
the fancy feed-forward network at the heart of these models work? Its power
rests on a few novel architectural innovations, which combine representational
width and representational depth with a special form of context awareness. All
the things we've seen about other neural networks still apply here. The
transformer is a kind of deep feed-forward network, which uses a huge amount of
training data. But the key innovation is that, within each ``layer'', the
transformer can develop many forms of context representation which relate all
the tokens in a context to each other.\footnote{The Simbrain simulation \simref{Language $>$ Tiny language model} is a small GPT-style transformer in which the residual stream, attention heads, and weight matrices can all be viewed as text is processed and as the model is trained.}

\subsection{Transformer Blocks and the Residual Stream}\label{transformerBlocks}

The transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention} is made up of layers
or ``blocks'', which are specialized to process the large contexts that are fed
to the network as input. With training, these blocks learn to find long-range
dependencies between different parts of a context. Recall that the context
includes an original prompt, its own response to that prompt, etc.; it includes
the \emph{entire exchange} you've had up to the current point, so long
as it fits in the context window. Each block combines one or more \glossary[attention head]{attention heads} with a standard feed-forward network or MLP (see figure \ref{transformerBlockSimple}).\footnote{MLP stands for multilayer perceptron, that is, a vanilla feed-forward network, and is a nod to the relevant history (section \extref{ageOfPerceptron}).}
Again, we maintain the ``row processing'' perspective of section
\extref{matrixMultiplication} throughout the discussion. That is, we treat the inputs to the transformer as a stack of vectors, each corresponding to one token in the context window. In the attention heads these vectors are compared with each other, and then the results of all this attentional processing are passed through the block's MLP, one vector at a time.

% More on normalization, "scaled dot product"
% Perhaps find a way to parse out the more difficult aspects of this
The attention heads are where the contextual awareness occurs. Each token
representation in a stack of inputs is compared to every previous one in the sequence. In our
simple example, of ``hello how are you?'', ``are'' is compared to ``hello'', ``how'', and itself.\footnote{It is not compared to future tokens ``you'' and ``?'', due to a causal mask, as discussed below.} These comparisons are done using matrices $\textbf{K}$ and $\textbf{Q}$, which stand for key and query. The flow of processing is illustrated in figure \ref{transformerBlockSimple}. The stacks of token representations entering the block are processed through the key and query matrices using matrix multiplication. The resulting stacks of key and query vectors are then compared to each other using a vector-comparison matrix.\footnote{A discussion of how vector comparison matrices are generated is in section \extref{vector_comparisons}; see in particular figure \extref{corrPlot}.} We then use a ``triangular'' or ``causal mask'' to remove entries that compare current tokens to future tokens.\footnote{That would amount to a kind of cheating given that we are trying to predict future tokens and don't want to use future information to do that.} The remaining values are then normalized. This is a matrix of \glossary{attention scores}. Note that this matrix is computed on the fly, at inference time, and is thus represented slightly differently (with a lighter outline) in the figures in this section. It looks like a changing triangular representation in Simbrain, as information is processed through the network. These scores reflect the  degree to which vector representations of tokens should be taken to be similar to each other for the purposes of a given head in a given layer of such a network. In geometric terms, to what extent should those tokens  be represented facing in the same direction in the embedding space?\footnote{We can think about the comparison of keys and queries as a kind of table lookup, where the query asks a kind of question, specifying what a given token is ``looking for'', and the key is a potential answer, advertising ``the kind of information available at those positions'' \cite{beckmann2025mechanistic}. Also see \weburl{https://e2eml.school/transformers.html}. The queries scan across past-token keys (due to the triangular mask that is used) and select information useful for the high-level task of predicting next tokens.} 

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\accessibleimage[scale=.25]{./images/transformerBlockResidualStream.png}{Transformer-block schematic. A residual stream rises through an attention head and an MLP; each component reads from the stream and adds its output back into it. The attention head contains key, query, and value inputs.}
\caption[Jeff Yoshimi and Pierre Beckmann, with consultation from Tim Meyer.]{(Left) One transformer
block, with a single attention head. The matrix (or tensor) of activations input to
the block is processed by three matrices: $\textbf{K}$, $\textbf{Q}$, and

matrix of self-attention scores. Since the scores are generated on the fly at inference time, they are shown with a lighter shade of gray. These scores are used to filter the value vectors that result when the input is processed through $\textbf{V}$. The MLP
typically involves a hidden node layer with a nonlinear activation function, that is larger than the input (hence the up
projection and down projection). A residual connection (or skip connection) connects the input to the attention block with its output, and another one connects the input to the MLP with its output. (Right) It can be useful to think of the residual connections as forming a single residual stream (the straight vertical line), which the attention heads and MLP read from and write to.}
\label{transformerBlockSimple}

The stack of token representations input to the block is also multiplied by a ``value'' matrix $\textbf{V}$, producing a stack of value vectors. The matrix of self-attention scores is then used to scale these value vectors. The attention scores determine how much of the value from a given past token should be passed along through the network \cite{beckmann2025mechanistic}. The value vectors carry the main information from the inputs, and the attention scores act as a kind of filter deciding which context information is important to be passed along within a given head at a given block. In a sense the filter learns what to focus on (hence ``self-attention'').

Once processed through the attention mechanism, activations from all the heads are combined and processed by a
traditional three-layer feed-forward network, an MLP, whose hidden layer is typically higher-dimensional than the input.  The MLP often contains more
weights than the rest of the block, and it plays an important role, enriching the filtered information from the heads with associative background information. It can be helpful to see the transformer
block as being made up of these two functional pieces: an
attention mechanism that specializes in identifying long-range dependencies in the
context, and an MLP sublayer that performs arbitrary operations (that can often be interpreted as ``associative knowledge enrichment'') on that curated information.  

% Add backwards ref to resnet discussion
There is one skip (or residual) connection that ``jumps'' from the input to the attention heads to their combined output, and then a second connection from the input to the output of the MLP. As a result we can think of the transformer block as reading
from and writing to a single \glossary{residual stream} of activations in a
latent space \cite{elhage2021mathematical, milliere2024philosophical2} whose
dimension is the embedding dimension (for a graphical introduction to these ideas, see section
\extref{flowDiagrams}).\footnote{The concept of a residual comes from statistics, where it describes how far data lies from a model, for example a regression line. The idea
here is that rather than transforming information completely from one layer to
the next,  the layers just produce ``deltas'' on a single stream of
information, which can thus be thought of as a residual stream, an informational bus that is updated via deltas.} This is the
perspective shown in the right panel of figure \ref{transformerBlockSimple}, and it's
a crucial perspective, because it means we can think of all the attention heads and MLPs in the network as operating on a single space. Think of a single flow of items flowing along a long central belt on an assembly line, being picked up and processed in different ways. The attention heads ``take the products off the belt'' and the MLPs modify them. As a stack of token representations moves upwards through the
residual stream, it is progressively enriched by the focusing and curating operations of the attention heads and by the addition of learned information via the MLPs.\footnote{Since we can think of these as reads and writes, this
also means that we can think in a more algorithmic or symbolic way about what
is happening in the network, with representations being shuffled and moved
around and erased, in a nod to older views of classical AI (we return to
this topic in section \extref{llmPhilosophy}).}

\subsection{Representational Depth and Width Revisited}\label{depthWidthRevisited}

We can get a useful lens on what LLMs learn by returning to the concepts of
representational width and representational depth introduced in chapter \extref{ch_intro}.  Roughly speaking, representational width corresponds to the representational capacity of a neural network at one stage of processing, and representational depth corresponds to the presence of many processing layers.
 
Representational width here corresponds to the use of a
``multi-head'' attention mechanism. This allows the network to pay attention to
different features of the token representations coming to it through the
residual stream (see figure \ref{multipleHeads}). The residual stream is projected into several lower-dimensional heads, each processing the input in parallel, and then projected back up to the residual stream and combined. For example, a 9-dimensional embedding might be split into three 3-dimensional heads.\footnote{Most implementations use an even split: the dimensionality of each head is often the embedding dimension divided by the number of heads.}
Multi-headed attention, in letting the
network use \emph{multiple} ways to scan over the context for relevance, is a
bit like convolutional networks (section \extref{convolutionalLayer})
developing \emph{multiple} filters to analyze an image (which we also described as
representational width).

Representational width also applies to the MLPs, which typically involve a projection to a \emph{higher}-dimensional hidden-unit space, to allow for more associative information to be encoded.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\accessibleimage[scale=.35]{./images/transformerBlockMultiHeadResidualStream.png}{Stacked transformer architecture with a shared residual stream. Each of k blocks contains several attention heads and an MLP; all read from and write additions to the vertical residual stream before softmax output.}
\caption[Jeff Yoshimi with consultation from Tim Meyer.]{Schematic of the
transformer architecture. Multiple transformer blocks are stacked, capturing
representational depth, as in a CNN (the number of layers is here given as $k$, but elsewhere in the chapter as $n_{\mbox{layers}}$). Each block contains a multi-head attention
mechanism, where each head learns to represent inputs to the block in a
different way, capturing representational width.}
\label{multipleHeads}
\end{figure}

Representational depth is achieved by stacking many of these
transformer blocks on top of each other ($k$ times in figure
\ref{multipleHeads}). As token representations move
through the layers of attention heads and MLPs, they acquire more and more
context information and are steadily transformed, with the vectors
corresponding to them in the latent space reoriented in slightly different
directions to reflect this refinement of meaning.\footnote{The 3Blue1Brown
video linked at the start of the chapter has useful visualizations of this
process, represented geometrically.}

Recall that with deep networks for vision,
we get features, features of features, features of these features, etc., whose
activations match neural response properties of different layers of the human
visual system. This builds on the old idea of the \emph{Pandemonium} model
(section \extref{cog_rev}), which involved (at successive layers): edge
detectors, detectors for combinations of edges, detectors for combinations of
these combinations (e.g., fragments of letters), and ultimately full letter
detectors. In a similar way, the successive layers of a transformer language
model correspond to increasingly complex features of the input stream,
including syntactic categories, semantic properties, and far more complex
features as well.\footnote{The extent to which activation patterns correspond
to syntactic or semantic features is measured using post hoc interpretation
techniques such as probing (see chapter \extref{ch_mechinterp}). As with so many other neural network features,
these were not ``programmed in'' by the engineers, but are emergent from the
network after training, and are studied and described by scientists after the
fact. What is known about what the layers do is discussed in section \extref{llmLinguisticStructure}.} However, one way the depth of transformers differs from earlier networks like convolutional networks is that a feature can be \emph{ignored} for many layers. So it's not a matter of simply combining and recombining features from earlier layers. It's more about the attention heads and MLPs grabbing a token representation when the time is right, enriching it with a bit of relevant information at that layer, and passing it back up along the residual stream. It will be advertised using key values at every head, but only where there is a strong match to a query will it receive attention and be operated on again.

The transformer architecture is trained using gradient descent and supervised
learning (chapter \extref{ch_supervised}). These are the same ideas used for a
simple feed-forward network trained using backprop, but with many more
trainable components. The error signals used in gradient descent are being
backpropagated through a \emph{lot} of stuff here! Residual connections are
especially important in this regard, as they allow the gradient to effectively flow
backward through the many layers of the transformer without disappearing or
exploding in value. Much of the art of
architectural design lies in giving a network both lots of structural latitude
to learn the links it needs to \emph{while also} having an architecture that can
efficiently train at scale.

We can summarize these ideas with extended quotes from one of the authors' papers \cite{beckmann2025mechanistic}. Think of the attention heads as being specialized to find and route information that is relevant at a given stage of processing:
\begin{quote}
The fundamental job of an attention head is to retrieve relevant information from the residual streams of previous tokens and integrate it into the residual stream embodying the model's evolving understanding of the current token... without context, ``bank'' might be taken to refer to a financial institution, but the nearby occurrence of ``water,'' ``mud,'' and ``fish'' indicates that the correct interpretation in this context is ``riverbank''... One attention head might specialize in retrieving adjective information. Its query might implicitly ask: ``What properties are being ascribed to the main subject?'' while its keys would advertise tokens that happen to be adjectives. These tokens would then get a strong match and the attention head would assign a strong attention score to them... This mechanism allows an LLM to figure out which features are most relevant for interpreting the current token in its particular context and to dynamically differentiate it from other contexts.
\end{quote}
Again, recall that information might \emph{not} be focused on for many layers of the network, but then at a given point it gets plucked out of the residual stream and becomes an object of interest. So attention heads allow for extremely long-range communication throughout the many layers of a network.

The MLPs take whatever the attention heads decide is important at a given layer (based on matches between keys and queries) and then enrich that information with associative information stored in the weights and biases of the MLPs. This is what classical feed-forward networks were so good at: building up these associative memory systems, as we have seen throughout much of the book. So it's like this old connectionist idea of an associative memory has been given this specific role in this stage of processing.\footnote{This is comparable to traditional ``dense'' feed-forward networks playing a role in the final layer of a CNN; see chapter \extref{ch_cnn}.} Beckmann and Queloz continue:
\begin{quote}
While attention heads are crucial for gathering and routing contextually relevant information, the multilayer perceptron (MLP) layers, which follow the attention layers in each transformer block, play an equally vital role in deepening conceptual understanding. If attention heads are about identifying and carrying over relevant information across residual streams, MLP layers perform computations within the current token's residual stream. They are largely about processing, refining, and enriching the information present in that stream... More precisely, MLPs can be seen as performing two functions. On the one hand, they combine the diverse pieces of information retrieved by attention layers. For instance, once an attention head has brought features for ``Golden Gate'' and ``Bridge'' into the current token's stream, an MLP layer must combine these distinct features to activate a higher-level ``Golden Gate Bridge'' feature more strongly. On the other hand, MLPs augment the resulting combination with associated knowledge recalled from the model's learned parameters
\end{quote}

In this way, the journey of a token's representation is a process of refinement and enrichment through many processing layers until at the final layer it is an extremely rich representation that packs in enough information to predict a next token, like ``Golden Gate Bridge in the context of a discussion of its history, in a conversation that is regarding it as beautiful, in the last position of a noun phrase that would normally be followed by a verb.''
