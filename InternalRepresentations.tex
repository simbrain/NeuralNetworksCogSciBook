\chapter{Representations in Neural Networks}\label{ch_representations}
\chapterauthor{Jeff Yoshimi}

In this chapter we discuss the representations that develop in neural networks. These representations are a central concern of two approaches emphasized in this book: connectionism, which uses neural networks to model psychological and behavioral features of the mind without much concern for neural realism (section \extref{connectionism}), and computational cognitive neuroscience, which uses them to model psychological and neural processes together (section \extref{ccn}). Recall from the introduction (chapter \extref{ch_intro}) that neural networks are trained, not programmed. They develop useful abilities and, as we will see, representations, without having anything programmed in, simply by learning to perform tasks from data. This was a source of a great deal of excitement in the early connectionist discussions of the 1980s (section \extref{first_resurgence}). After the deep-learning revolution (section \extref{deep_revolution}), there was renewed interest in these systems, since deep networks for vision did so well at predicting activity in the primate visual system. More recently, attention has turned to transformers and their representations of language.\footnote{These issues are back at the forefront of neural network research as of the 2020s, and are studied (especially as they apply to the transformer architecture) in the field of mechanistic interpretability, which is discussed in chapter \extref{ch_mechinterp}.} Across many domains, the representations that neural networks learn are a topic of live interest.

We explore the idea first conceptually and then historically, since it has been a throughline of neural network theorizing for decades. We begin by saying what a representation is and how representations can be pictured as points in activation spaces. We then develop what we call Zipser's dictum, based on an important early analysis of how neural networks can tell us about psychology and the brain even when they are not biologically realistic. The rest of the chapter works through case studies, from early connectionist models of reading aloud and of grammar, through methods for comparing networks with brains, to deep networks whose layers line up with stages of the primate visual system.

A representation in a neural network is a state (in the sense of ``state'' discussed in chapter \extref{ch_dst},  a pattern of activation over a set of nodes) that reliably occurs in response to some external input. For example, whatever activations occur in a neural network when an apple is present can be thought of as being a representation of an apple (figure \ref{repStateSpace}). The same idea applies to biological neural networks: a pattern of activity across a population of neurons is a point in a neural state space, and the patterns that reliably occur in response to an apple can be thought of as the brain's representation of an apple. Note that a representation can be spread across many nodes, so that we have the input representation and hidden-unit representations, for example.\footnote{The Simbrain simulation \simref{Tutorials $>$ Localist and distributed representations} compares representations that use one node per object with representations spread across many nodes.} The details of what a representation is---and whether they are even valuable to posit---are highly contested, but we assume this simple definition here.\footnote{Doubts about representations are associated with embodied cognition and with examples such as Braitenberg vehicles, which show that one can have intelligent behaviors in a body-world system without there being much value to interpreting internal states. Those who allow representations often add other components to the definition of a representation. For example, it should be possible to activate a representational state absent its normal cause (representations are ``detachable'' rather than ``stimulus bound''), which allows for cases of misrepresentation, planning in the absence of the object, and so on. It is generally assumed that  representations are used by the agent to guide its behavior. Many assume that computational processes must transform representations into other representations, to support adaptive behaviors.}

Representations can also have different levels of generality. In figure \ref{repStateSpace}, each apple produces its own point, a representation of that particular apple. But the apples' points are near each other, so the region they occupy can be thought of as a representation of apples in general. The same goes for the bananas. A larger region containing both, the large oval in the figure, can be thought of as a representation of fruit. It is far from the region for animals, where the kittens are. The result is a nested structure, which we will see again in Elman's network below.

Representations can be understood using the visual language we have been developing for understanding the mind, in terms of points or vectors in high-dimensional spaces. In section \extref{sect_xor_remap} we saw that multilayer feed-forward networks trained by supervised learning methods (\eg backprop) will remap an input space in order to make a linearly inseparable problem separable. That was an abstract example, but it also applies to more everyday cases. Suppose a network is trained to recognize letters in images (figure \ref{remappingClusters}). Each point corresponds to a representation of one image. In the input space, uppercase ``A'' and lowercase ``a'' produce separate clusters, since they look quite different. Lowercase ``a'' looks more like ``o'' than like ``A'', so the cluster for ``o'' lies between them, and there is no way to draw a line that separates the A's from the o's.\footnote{So a network with no hidden layer, like the perceptron, could not learn to sort these letters. This is the kind of limitation that Minsky and Papert identified in perceptrons \cite{minsky1969perceptrons} (chapter \extref{ch_lms_backprop}).} In the hidden-unit space, the two clusters of A's are merged into one, separate from the o's. The network has learned to treat inputs that look different as the same, and inputs that look similar as different.

One way to understand how these representations work is that they involve remapping and recombining patterns in the input space in various ways. In some cases, as in the one shown in the figure, separate clusters get merged at another layer.  We saw a related idea in Selfridge's Pandemonium model, which also recognized letters (chapter \extref{ch_history}): each layer combines representations from the layer before it to produce more complex features. The same idea also comes up in the ``stacked'' layers of a deep network (chapter \extref{ch_cnn}).

The structure of a network shapes the representations it can develop (section \extref{structureNets} and figure \ref{repWidthDepth}). The wider a layer, the more representations it can store: a layer with more nodes has a higher-dimensional state space, with room for more distinct representations. This is the layer's \glossary{representational width}.\footnote{Compare the linear representation hypothesis in mechanistic interpretability, according to which representations are directions in an activation space (chapter \extref{ch_mechinterp}).} The deeper a network, the more layers of representations it has, each built from the representations of the layer before it. This is the network's \glossary{representational depth}.

\section{Zipser's Dictum and the Question of Realism}\label{zipserDictum}

We have seen that supervised learning methods are not generally taken to be neurally realistic in their mechanistic details, since there is no obvious way for error to be sent ``backward'' across synapses and along axons, which would require each neuron to have access to the weights of the neurons it projects to (section \extref{backprop}).\footnote{However, some circuits have been identified in the brain that may implement error-based supervised learning, \eg climbing fibers in the cerebellum (see chapter \extref{ch_neuro}). Error-based learning that is similar in some ways to backprop is also emphasized in predictive coding accounts of the brain and predictive processing accounts of cognition. For a recent discussion of neural realism of backprop, see \cite{whittington2019theories}.}  However, even if backprop is not realistic, it produces representations that are similar to representations humans use. As David Zipser asked, ``Why should model neurons, particularly the `hidden units' which are not directly instructed what to do, develop response properties that so closely resemble real neurons?'' This is especially surprising, he notes, ``because most of the algorithms used to train the networks are not biologically plausible'' \cite{zipser1992identification}. % TODO: add page numbers for both quotes
We will call this observation \glossary{Zipser's dictum}: training a network on the right task can produce realistic representations, even when the network and its learning algorithm are not biologically realistic. Even if backprop does not happen in most parts of the brain, it can still be used as a device to discover the kinds of representations that the brain finds. How the brain develops these representations is still a mystery, but backprop lets us at least see what those representations might look like and what function they might serve.

% "Perhaps the variation between individuals of a species is of the same kind as the variation between different instances of the same identification model."  Learning finds a viable model. "Given the conjecture that all the possible models are similar, we might expect that any unbiased optimization procedure would generate about the same set of models. Thus, to a first approximation, the final results should not be dependent on the learning procedure. In particular, it should not be necessary to mimic biological development and learning to get veridical models." "correct results do not require realistic learning procedures that simulate the way the brain develops and adapts."  "The neural systems identification paradigm provides a systematic way to generate realistic models starting with a high-level description of a hypothesized computation and some architectural and physiological constraints about the area being modeled....This approach eliminates the need to find neural implementation algorithms by ad hoc means, because it uses optimization techniques that work automatically once the problem has been set up." Then we add constraints. "additional physiological constraints can be added until the model behavior mimics the experimental data."
Zipser's own explanation was that a task, together with basic architectural constraints, allows only a narrow family of workable solutions. If all the models that can perform the task are similar to one another, then any unbiased optimization procedure should find roughly the same ones, so the learning procedure should not matter much. In particular, it should not be necessary to mimic biological development and learning to arrive at accurate models. He even suggested that the variation between individual brains of a species might be like the variation between different trained instances of the same model.\footnote{On this view, backprop is a tool for what Zipser called neural systems identification: start with a hypothesized computation and some architectural and physiological constraints, train a network to perform the computation, and then add further physiological constraints until the model's behavior matches experimental data \cite{zipser1992identification}.}

A comparison with evolution helps explain why. Evolution also searches a space of possible solutions, keeping variations that do better and discarding those that do worse. Neither evolution nor gradient descent (section \extref{sect_gradient_descent}) finds a single best solution. Each settles into solutions that are good enough, like the local minima of an error surface (figure \extref{local_minima}). If a task constrains the solutions strongly enough (in supervised learning, the constraints are the input-target pairs a network must learn), the good-enough solutions will resemble one another. Any process that searches for them, whether evolution, development, or backprop, should then find roughly the same ones.

The examples in the rest of this chapter all illustrate Zipser's dictum. They show how, under the pressure of gradient descent, networks trained by backprop and its variants produce realistic internal representations. In NETtalk and Elman's networks, the representations are psychologically realistic, which supports connectionism (section \extref{connectionism}). In deep vision networks, they are also neurally realistic, which supports computational cognitive neuroscience (section \extref{ccn}).

\section{NETtalk}


% 7×29 one-hot letters; outputs = 21 articulatory features + 5 stress/syllable-boundary units. No reason to have separation in the inputs since it's just one hot letters. NETtalk \cite{sejnowski1987parallel} was a model of reading English words aloud, created by Terrence Sejnowski and Charles Rosenberg in 1987. The network is a three-layer feed-forward network with the structure shown in figure \ref{net_talk}. The input layer codes for a moving window of letters, one of which is taken to be the current input and the rest of which provide the network with information about neighboring letters.\footnote{Each letter is coded by a 29-dimensional vector (involving one-hot, one-of-29 representations of particular letters, with three additional nodes representing punctuation and word boundaries).} The hidden layer has 80 hidden units. The output layer has 26 units: 21 encode articulatory features of phonemes, such as place of articulation, voicing, and vowel height, and five encode stress and syllable-boundary information.\footnote{An interactive recreation of this simulation is the Simbrain simulation \simref{Language $>$ NETtalk}; see the related speech synthesizer documentation at \weburl{https://docs.simbrain.net/docs/worlds/speechsynthesizer.html}.} It was presented with written letters in English and was trained to pronounce those letters. The standard network had 18,629 weights and biases. In one dictionary experiment, a 120-hidden-unit variant trained on the 1000 most common words in a 20,012-word dictionary generalized to the full dictionary at 77 percent accuracy without additional training; with continued training on the dictionary, performance reached 90 percent after five passes. The network was shown to perform well with noisy input and to gracefully degrade \cite{sejnowski1987parallel}.

Here is the key point: the hidden units learned to produce a complete separation of consonants and vowels, even though the network was not told about consonants or vowels. That is, when the central input was a consonant, the hidden-unit activation was in one part of the hidden-unit space; when it was a vowel, it was in another region. Different vowels in the context of different letters produced different points but as a group they were all near each other. Similarly for consonants. The network was not told about the difference between vowels and consonants---it simply learned these categories while it was trained on the pronunciation task \cite{sejnowski1987parallel}. This showed how in learning a mapping from sensory inputs to motor outputs, psychologically meaningful categories could take form in a network's hidden-unit space.

One striking feature of this example is that the sounds the network produced could be artificially synthesized and played back. You can ``hear'' the network learn (listen for yourself at \weburl{https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gakJlr3GecE}). Initially the network produced a babble of sounds, like a baby, but it eventually produced reasonably fluent (albeit somewhat creepy) speech \cite{sejnowski1987parallel}. This is Zipser's dictum at work: even if backprop is not neurally plausible, the network seems to be doing something \emph{like} what the brain does, and it does so by developing psychologically realistic representations of vowels and consonants.

\section{Elman's Prediction Networks}

%  Mention Simbrain sims (maybe with a screenshot)

Another early-connectionist analysis of internal representations occurred in the context of Jeff Elman's simple recurrent networks (see chapter \extref{ch_supervised_recurrent}). In a famous paper \cite{elman1990finding}, Jeff Elman trained a simple recurrent network like the one in figure \ref{elman_srn_categories} to predict the next word in a sentence.\footnote{\weburl{http://psych.colorado.edu/~kimlab/Elman1990.pdf}.}  The input data used to train the network consisted of thousands of sentences generated using a simple ``caveman'' grammar. Some sample sentences generated by this grammar are shown in figure \ref{elman_sentences}. The network predicts the next word in a sentence at any time. For example, it predicts that the word ``cat'' will be followed by ``chase'' or ``eat''. With training, error can be reduced, but it never goes to zero, because a given word can be followed by more than one other word. Some words are more likely than others to follow one another, and the predictions the network makes match these patterns. It also learns some general rules, like expecting verbs after nouns \cite{elman1990finding}.\footnote{A Simbrain simulation that partially replicates the results of these papers is \simref{Language $>$ Next-word prediction (SRN)}.}

\centering
\frame{\accessibleimage[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/elman_sentences.png}{Two columns of simple training sentences for Elman's word-prediction network, such as mouse move book, cat move book, woman break, and monster smash plate.}}
\caption[Generated by Jeff Yoshimi based on \cite{elman1990finding}.]{Some of the sentences used to train the word-prediction network.}
\label{elman_sentences}
\end{figure}

After being trained to predict the next word in a simple text corpus (using the same type of \glossary{auto-regression} used in transformers like GPT; see chapter \extref{ch_transformers}), the network's internal states were studied. After training, the average response of the network to different words was tested, and it was found that similar words were near each other in the hidden-unit space. This can be seen in figure \ref{elman_srn_categories}, which can be thought of as a dimensional reduction of the hidden-unit space.\footnote{The original picture this was adapted from was generated by exposing the network to each word in the context of many sentences (\eg ``eat'', ``cat''), and then taking the average hidden-unit activation across these exposures. The resulting vectors are like the centers of clusters in the hidden-unit space.}  Each point in the right panel of the figure corresponds to a vector (an activation pattern) in the hidden-unit space, projected to two dimensions. Distances between points are meaningful: points closer to each other in the diagram correspond to hidden-unit vectors that are closer to each other in the hidden-unit space.

Grammatically and semantically similar words are near each other in the hidden-unit space and they form into a hierarchical structure. Nouns produce hidden-unit representations that are similar to one another and which form into one cluster, and similarly for verbs. Within the nouns we see further clusters for animate vs. inanimate nouns, and there are additional categories as well.\footnote{There are two categories of transitive verbs because some verbs (like ``chase'') were always transitive in the data (that is, taking a direct object), while others (like ``break'') were sometimes transitive, sometimes not.}  The striking thing about this network was that it learned these categories \emph{without being told anything about grammar}. None of the categories apparent in its hidden-unit space were directly programmed into the network: it simply learned them in the process of solving the input-output task it was given (compare the way NETtalk learned phonological categories when learning to read aloud, or the way deep networks develop realistic edge detectors and other feature detectors when trained to recognize objects).
% Even down to food on one side and glass and late near each other

The network was used to argue against the prevailing view in psycholinguistics, associated with Noam Chomsky, that grammars are innate, rather than learned \cite{chomsky1965aspects}.  Elman's work showed that a network with no built-in grammar could acquire the rudiments of a grammar from the environment. Its grammatical categories were not ``built in'' to the network, but learned from the training data---the pattern of words fed to the network.\footnote{This argument was developed at length in \cite{elman1996rethinking}. Critics such as Gary Marcus responded that networks like this fail to generalize grammatical rules beyond their training data the way people do \cite{marcus1998rethinking}. Connectionists replied that such findings are compatible with statistical learning, and that with suitable representations networks can generalize without abstract rules \cite{mcclelland1999does}.}

\section{Comparing Networks and Brains}\label{compareNetsBrains}

So far we have focused on cases where we could visualize clusters in a hidden-unit space and judge for ourselves whether they are psychologically meaningful. Comparing an \glossary{artificial neural network} to a \glossary{biological neural network} takes more, and this begins to take us from connectionism to computational neuroscience and computational cognitive neuroscience. The two systems are very different. A layer of a deep network might have thousands of nodes, while a brain area has millions of neurons, recorded with electrodes or fMRI. There is no way to match node to neuron. What can be compared is the relationships among states. Recall that in Elman's network, distances between points in the hidden-unit space are meaningful: similar words produce nearby points. The same is true in the brain: similar stimuli tend to produce similar patterns of neural activity. This section describes the two main methods for making these comparisons.

\subsection{Representational Similarity Analysis}\label{rsa}

One method asks whether two systems agree about which stimuli are similar. This is the idea behind \emph{representational similarity analysis} (RSA) \cite{kriegeskorte2008representational}. The same set of stimuli, say a set of images, is presented to both systems. For each system, we measure how similar its responses are for every pair of stimuli. The result is a table of pairwise similarities for each system (figure \ref{rsaComparison}). If two faces produce similar states in one system and similar states in the other, and a face and a car produce different states in both, the two tables will look alike. The tables can be compared directly, even though the systems have different numbers of units and are made of different stuff.

Arrows lead from each state space to a 3 by 3 table of pairwise similarities, with rows and columns labeled by the apple, apple, and banana pictures. The two tables have nearly the same shading, and a double-headed arrow connects them.}
\caption[Jeff Yoshimi.]{Representational similarity analysis. The same stimuli, two apples and a banana, are presented to an artificial neural network (top) and to a biological one (bottom). A classic study used RSA to compare two brains \cite{kriegeskorte2008matching}. Humans and monkeys viewed the same 92 photographs of faces, bodies, animals, plants, and objects. The study focused on IT, the last stage of the ventral visual stream, which is involved in object recognition (figure \extref{brain_vision}). In the humans, activity in IT was measured with fMRI; in the monkeys, it was recorded from individual neurons with electrodes. The two species' similarity tables turned out to be strikingly alike (figure \ref{rsaHumanMonkey}). In both, images of animate things (people and animals) produced similar patterns, distinct from the patterns for inanimate things, and faces formed a cluster of their own within the animate group. This is the kind of nested structure described at the start of the chapter (figure \ref{repStateSpace}), found in two species measured in two different ways. As we will see, RSA can compare networks to brains in the same way.

The idea goes back to what Shepard and Chipman called \emph{second-order isomorphism} \cite{shepard1970second}. A representation need not resemble what it represents. The representation of a red patch is not red, and the representation of a square is not square. What matters is that relations among representations mirror relations among the things represented. If red is more similar to orange than to blue, the representation of red should be more similar to the representation of orange than to the representation of blue. The same goes for comparing two systems: a network and a brain can represent the world in compatible ways without their states resembling each other at all.

\subsection{Encoding and Decoding Models}\label{encodingDecoding}

The other main method is to predict one system's states from the other's. In \emph{model-to-brain encoding}, a pattern of neural activity is predicted from the pattern of activations in a network layer (figure \ref{encodingModel}).\footnote{The terms \emph{encoding} and \emph{decoding} come from neuroscience, where neurons are said to encode features of a stimulus, and other parts of the brain decode their activity to guide behavior. In vision, encoding runs from the image through areas like those shown in figure \ref{ventralStreamNetwork}. The terms are used in many other ways too, in neuroscience and in machine learning. Here they name the direction of prediction: from model to brain, or from brain to model.} The same stimuli are presented to the network and to the brain. For each recorded neuron (or fMRI voxel), a separate weighted sum of the layer's activations is fit to that neuron's responses, and the fitted model is then tested on new stimuli. Each fitted model takes a whole vector of activations and predicts a single value; together, the models for all the recorded neurons predict a whole pattern. If the predictions succeed, the layer carries the information the neurons respond to. In the terms of mechanistic interpretability, model-to-brain encoding uses a linear probe (section \extref{linearProbes}) whose target is brain activity rather than a property like part of speech.

Model-to-brain encoding also depends on relations among states lining up, but not the same relations that RSA uses. A linear map preserves which states lie between which, but it can stretch some directions of the network's activation space and ignore others. So a network can predict a brain area well even when its overall similarity structure, the thing RSA measures, differs from the brain's, and the two methods can disagree \cite{kornblith2019similarity}. Diedrichsen and Kriegeskorte show how the two approaches are related within a common framework \cite{diedrichsen2017representational}.

Decoding runs in the other direction. In \emph{brain-to-model decoding}, a network's activations are predicted from brain activity. More generally, a decoder can predict anything from a system's activity, such as the stimulus or what the subject will do. Applied to a network, a decoder is just a linear probe. Applied to a brain, it reveals what information a region carries. For example, a simple weighted sum of the activity of a few hundred sites in monkey IT can identify which object the monkey is looking at, within about a tenth of a second of the image appearing \cite{hung2005fast}, and readouts like this predict how well people recognize the same objects \cite{majaj2015simple}. Decoders can also be combined with deep networks. In one study of brain-to-model decoding, decoders were trained to predict a deep network's layer activations from fMRI, and the predicted activations were then used to identify which object a person was seeing or imagining, including kinds of objects the decoders had never been trained on \cite{horikawa2017generic}.\footnote{Decoding is used widely beyond these comparisons. Brain-computer interfaces decode activity in motor cortex to move a cursor or a robotic arm \cite{hochberg2006neuronal}. In consciousness research, decoders help infer what a person perceives without asking them to report it, so that brain activity related to reporting is not mistaken for activity related to consciousness (``no-report paradigms'') \cite{tsuchiya2015no}.}

All three methods work for the same basic reason. An artificial neural network and a biological one are completely different in detail, as Zipser emphasized, but the relations among their states can still be similar: stimuli that produce similar patterns in one produce similar patterns in the other. RSA compares those relations directly, and encoding and decoding exploit them to map one set of patterns onto the other. So these methods can show that a network and a brain carry similar information. They do not by themselves show that the two process that information in the same way. Model-to-brain encoding has also been used to compare large language models with the brain (section \extref{llmNeuro}).

\section{Deep Vision Networks}\label{deepVisionNets}

As discussed in chapter \extref{ch_history}, after the deep-learning revolution, neural networks came back in fashion, and scientists once again began turning to these as models of brain and cognition (for example, see the work of Yamins and DiCarlo \cite{yamins2014performance, yamins2016using}). Deep networks (chapter \extref{ch_cnn}) trained using backprop to recognize objects in images produce activations that match neural responses in such brain areas as V1, V2, V4, and inferior temporal cortex (IT), which are discussed in chapter \extref{ch_neuro}.  V1 and V2 are part of early visual processing, with V1 containing edge detectors. IT is part of the ventral stream (also discussed in chapter \extref{ch_neuro}), which is involved in object recognition. The layers of these networks line up with stages of the visual pathway \cite{gucclu2015deep}: early layers, which develop edge detectors, best predict early visual areas like V1; intermediate layers best predict intermediate areas like V4; and top layers best predict IT. This correspondence between network layers and brain areas is shown in figure \ref{ventralStreamNetwork}.

These correspondences have been tested with both of the methods described above. In one study using model-to-brain encoding, monkeys viewed thousands of images of objects from eight categories (such as faces, cars, and animals) on cluttered backgrounds, while electrodes recorded from sites in V4 and IT. The top layer of the researchers' network predicted about half of the explainable variance in IT responses, nearly twice as much as earlier models \cite{yamins2014performance}. Figure \ref{yamins_2016_responses} shows the prediction for a single site in IT. The studies described in this section used different networks, but all were deep convolutional networks trained to recognize objects. RSA tells the same story. Using the 92 images described above, a deep network trained to recognize objects matched the similarity structure of human and monkey IT better than the other models tested \cite{khaligh2014deep}.

This is perhaps the most striking illustration of Zipser's dictum. For years, researchers built custom models of the visual system based on how the brain works. But these bespoke models were outperformed by networks simply optimized to recognize objects \cite{yamins2014performance}, including networks that originated in industrial applications \cite{cadieu2014deep}. Under the pressure of the gradient-descent algorithm alone, they develop realistic representations.

Convolutional networks extend Zipser's dictum from the learning algorithm to the architecture. A convolutional layer slides one shared filter across the whole image (section \extref{convolutionalLayer}), and there is no literal counterpart to this weight sharing in the brain (section \extref{cnn_applications}). So both the learning rule and part of the architecture are unrealistic, and yet the representations that develop are realistic. As Zipser suggested, the task and a few basic constraints, such as a hierarchy of layers with local receptive fields, seem to do most of the work.

\section{Other Examples}

The case studies above all use feed-forward or simple recurrent networks trained by backprop. Realistic representations also develop in other architectures, trained in other ways, and they are realistic in different ways. In a Hopfield network trained with a Hebbian rule, memories are stored as fixed-point attractors, and a partial or noisy input settles into the nearest stored pattern, much as a fragment of a memory can bring back the whole (chapter \extref{ch_unsupervised_recurrent}). Here the realism lies in the dynamics, in how states change over time. In a self-organizing map, nodes that respond to similar inputs end up near each other, producing topographic maps like those in the sensory areas of the cortex (chapter \extref{ch_unsupervised}). Here the realism lies in the spatial layout of the representations, not just their similarity structure.

Large language models extend the story to language (chapter \extref{ch_llm_cogsci}). Like Elman's network, they are trained to predict words from their context, with no grammar built in, and like Elman's network they learn grammatical structure anyway, including subject-verb agreement and hierarchical sentence structure (section \extref{llmLinguisticStructure}). Their internal states also predict brain activity in people reading or listening to the same text, as measured with the model-to-brain encoding described above (section \extref{llmNeuro}). LLMs thus provide psychological and neural evidence together, much as deep vision networks did for the visual system.

The methods used in this chapter, such as looking for clusters in a hidden-unit space or comparing similarity structures, were fairly simple in early connectionist work. In mechanistic interpretability (chapter \extref{ch_mechinterp}), they are being developed into a more systematic toolbox, mostly for studying transformers. A linear probe trains a simple classifier to read a property, such as part of speech, off a layer's activations (section \extref{linearProbes}), a more precise version of what Elman did when he looked for clusters of nouns and verbs. Sparse autoencoders break a layer's activations down into many separate features. Activation addition and ablations go further, changing a network's internal states to see how its behavior changes (sections \extref{activationAddition} and \extref{ablations}). This tests whether a representation is actually used to guide behavior, which, as noted at the start of the chapter, many take to be part of what makes a state a representation.
