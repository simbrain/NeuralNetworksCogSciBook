Convolutional neural networks (or CNNs) are a prominent type of deep neural network. They are a kind of many-layered network that is often used for processing visual inputs, like recognizing patterns in images or movies, though they have broader applications. They make use of a special kind of mapping between node layers, a convolutional layer.

The idea of re-mapping an input space to a hidden unit space in order to solve classification and regression problems is what drew neural networks out of their first ``dark age'' and back into the limelight, giving rise to an explosion of interest in neural networks in the 1980s and 1990s (chapter \extref{ch_history}). The internal representations of these networks---usually 3-node-layer networks trained by backprop---allowed them to solve previously unsolvable problems like XOR (figure \extref{xor_remapping}). These representations were often psychologically realistic (chapter \extref{ch_representations}). However, recall that a second winter lay in wait, as machine learning models became more prominent in the late 1990s and 2000s. What got us out of that second winter was \glossary[deep network]{deep networks}, that is, networks with more than 3 node layers, trained using new tricks and techniques, like graphics processing units for fast parallel computation, the ReLU activation function discussed in chapter \extref{ch_act_functions}, and the convolutional layers discussed in this chapter. These advances made it possible to use the same type of feed-forward network discussed in chapter \extref{ch_lms_backprop} on much more difficult problems.\footnote{This history is well told by Kurenkov in part 3 of \weburl{https://github.com/skynettoday/skynet-today/blob/master/_posts/overviews/2020-09-27-a-brief-history-of-neural-nets-and-deep-learning.md}. As he summarizes, ``Deep Learning = Lots of training data + Parallel Computation + Scalable, smart algorithms.''} Convolutional networks, though they date back to the early 1980s (section \ref{cnn_applications}), were among the most prominent of these deep networks. A landmark was AlexNet, a deep convolutional network that won the 2012 ImageNet image-recognition competition by a wide margin \cite{krizhevsky2012imagenet}.

In terms of engineering, these convolutional networks have been associated with improvements in image recognition, speech recognition, language translation, and in many other areas \cite{lecun2015deep, goodfellow2016deep}. They do this by creating hierarchies of representations, corresponding  to increasingly complex features of an input image. As we saw in chapter \extref{ch_neuro} (see figure \extref{deepLearning_Vision}) when such networks are trained to recognize images, they develop internal representations that are extremely similar to those developed by the human visual  system. Thus they are relevant both to neuroscience (where they can describe the behavior of neurons in the visual system), and to psychology (where they can describe internal representations humans might rely on).

The topic of convolutional networks is quite involved, and the field is active and continues to grow. Here we will describe some of the main concepts and some of their applications to neuroscience and psychology.

\section{Convolutional Layers}\label{convolutionalLayer}

% Terminology: source layer, source image, pixel array, input volume, output volume

The key idea with a convolutional network is to use a special type of weight layer called a \glossary{convolutional layer} to efficiently learn to recognize features in a previous layer.\footnote{An outstanding visual discussion of the concept of convolution is the 3Blue1Brown video at \weburl{https://youtu.be/KuXjwB4LzSA}.} Until now we've been dealing with weight layers that connect all the nodes in a source layer to all the nodes in a target layer. These are called \glossary[dense layer]{dense layers}, or fully connected layers. By contrast, convolutional layers involve a set of weights that is scanned, or convolved, over the source layer's activations to produce activations in a target layer. This operation is called a convolution of the source layer activations. Networks that feature convolutional layers are called \glossary[convolutional neural network]{convolutional neural networks}, or CNNs.
% Footnote on the mathematical definition of convolution, and how from that standpoint the source node activations are a function from index entries to activations.

The weights in a convolutional layer are organized into filters. A \glossary{filter} is a small set of weights that is scanned over the source layer to produce an output matrix. The source layer is often a 2d array, prototypically an image, so we can think of its activations as pixels and of the source layer as a pixel array (even when the input is not an image, this language is useful). The filter starts at the top left of the pixel array and moves from left to right and top to bottom.\footnote{Animations help here. A good place to start is the first animated gif at \weburl{https://stanford.edu/~shervine/teaching/cs-230/cheatsheet-convolutional-neural-networks}. In practice the dot products are computed in parallel rather than one at a time.} At each position, the dot product (see chapter \extref{ch_linear_algebra}) is taken between the filter and the patch of the pixel array it is on top of, which is called its \emph{receptive field}.\footnote{The term is borrowed from neuroscience, where a neuron's receptive field is the region of the visual field or other sensory input that activates it; see chapter \extref{ch_neuro}.} That is, each weight in the filter is multiplied by the pixel under it, and the products are added up. Recall that the dot product is something like a similarity score: the more the filter and the pixels match, the larger it is. Each dot product populates one activation in the target layer, which is called a \glossary{feature map}. So a convolutional layer computes a kind of \emph{sliding dot product}, and the feature map shows where the filter matches the pixel array.

Figure \ref{cnn_filter} shows an example.\footnote{Python code for editing a small filter and input image, to get a feel for how they produce a feature map, is at \weburl{https://colab.research.google.com/drive/1ywr3z8HRXYNPK-vd34kAATUPVWwwFqQE?usp=sharing}.} The left panel gives the general idea, and the right panel includes all the numbers, so you can check the computations. The filter is an edge detector, which responds to edges at a $-45^\circ$ angle, that is, edges shaped like a backslash `\textbackslash'. Since the image and the filter are both binary, each dot product just counts how many of the filter's $1$'s line up with $1$'s in the receptive field. In the highlighted position, all three line up with the left side of the smile, so the corresponding entry in the feature map is $3$, its largest value. Where the filter only partly matches, it produces smaller values. So the feature map gives a sense of where backslash-shaped edges occur in the image. Try working out a few of the other values, and what would happen if the filter or the image were changed.

A different filter detects a different feature. Figure \ref{cnn_filter2} repeats the example on the left and, on the right, applies a filter that detects edges shaped like a forward slash `/' to the same image. With this filter the largest value, 3, appears where the filter lines up with the right side of the smile, and the whole feature map is a mirror image of the one in figure \ref{cnn_filter}. Each filter produces its own feature map, showing where its feature occurs in the input. Try imagining other filters for this image, such as a horizontal line detector or a detector for the eyes, and what their feature maps would look like. Most convolutional layers use many filters (section \ref{sect_filterBank}).

Notice that the feature map is smaller than the image. Across a row of 9 pixels, a filter 3 pixels wide fits in $9 - 3 + 1 = 7$ positions, so the feature map is $7 \times 7$. In general, when the filter moves one pixel at a time and stays inside the image, the size of the output along each dimension (height and width) is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sliding_window}
\text{Output size} = \text{Input size} - \text{Filter size} + 1
\end{equation}

This is quite different from the weight layers we have studied so far: there are no fixed connections between particular nodes. Instead, the same small set of weights is reused at every position of the input, which is known as \glossary{weight sharing}.\footnote{There is no literal analog in the brain of sliding a filter over an input. However, the brain achieves something similar; see section \ref{cnn_applications}.} As a result, all that must be trained in figure \ref{cnn_filter} is $3 \times 3 = 9$ weights, rather than the $81 \times 49 = 3969$ weights that a dense layer from the input to the feature map would need. This efficiency is part of what made it possible to train such large networks.\footnote{As with ordinary weight layers, a bias is usually added to each dot product, and the result is passed through an activation function, typically ReLU (chapter \extref{ch_act_functions}). The examples and exercises in this chapter leave these steps out to keep the arithmetic simple.} And like other weights, filters are learned rather than programmed (chapter \extref{ch_supervised}; more on this in section \ref{sect_filterBank}).

\section{Stride and Padding}\label{sect_stride}

This section describes two settings that change the size of a feature map. It can be skipped: unless stated otherwise, convolutional layers in the rest of the chapter use the defaults of a stride of 1 and no padding, so equation \ref{eq:sliding_window} applies.

The number of pixels the filter moves at each step is called the \glossary{stride}. So far the stride has been 1. A larger stride skips positions and so produces a smaller output. Strides can differ in different directions, but we assume they are the same in all directions.

Since the filter cannot extend past the edges of the input, pixels near the edges fall in fewer receptive fields than pixels in the middle, and the output is smaller than the input. To handle this, \glossary{padding} can be added: extra zeros around the edges of the input. With no padding (sometimes called ``valid'' padding), the output shrinks, as in figure \ref{cnn_filter}. With a stride of 1 and a filter of odd width $F$, adding $(F-1)/2$ rows and columns of zeros on each side keeps the output the same size as the input (``same'' padding).\footnote{This page contains an interactive tool for exploring these ideas: \weburl{https://distill.pub/2019/computing-receptive-fields/}.}

Taking stride and padding into account, the output size along each dimension is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sliding_window_general}
\text{Output size} = \left\lfloor \frac{\text{Input size} - \text{Filter size} + 2 \times \text{Padding}}{\text{Stride}} \right\rfloor + 1
\end{equation}
where Padding is the number of rows or columns of zeros added to each side. The brackets $\lfloor \; \rfloor$ mean rounding down, since the filter can only fit a whole number of positions; any leftover pixels at the edges are not included in the output. With a stride of 1 and no padding, this reduces to equation \ref{eq:sliding_window}. For example, a $9 \times 9$ input with a $3 \times 3$ filter, a stride of 2, and no padding gives an output of size $\lfloor (9 - 3)/2 \rfloor + 1 = 4$ along each dimension, so the output is $4 \times 4$. With a stride of 1 and a padding of 1, the output is $(9 - 3 + 2)/1 + 1 = 9$, the same size as the input. The same formula applies to pooling layers (section \ref{sect_pooling}), with the pooling window in place of the filter.

\section{Applying a Filter to a Volume}\label{sect_filterVolume}

So far we have considered an artificial example where the input, filter, and feature map are matrices. However, the power of CNNs is that they can deal well with more complex tensors. In most applications of convolutional networks, the input is a volume: a stack of matrices, that is, a rank-3 tensor or 3d array (see section \extref{sect_tensors}). For example, a color image is usually stored as three channels, which hold its red, green, and blue values (for a fun example that makes this clear, see figure \ref{rgbVolume}). Volumes don't just occur as inputs to a CNN; they also arise inside the network. For example, stacking the feature maps of figures \ref{cnn_filter} and \ref{cnn_filter2} gives a $2 \times 7 \times 7$ volume (following the convention of chapter \extref{ch_linear_algebra}, shapes are given in the order channels, height, width).

If activations are tensors, then weights must be too, since they must match the volumes they are applied to. So we can think of filters as volumes that act on other volumes, as in figure \ref{filterToVolume}. We imagine moving the filter ``through'' the input volume, top-to-bottom and left-to-right, and at each stage of this operation we take the dot product of the filter and the underlying receptive field. Here the 27 cuboids in the Rubik's-cube-like filter are multiplied by 27 components of the input volume, and all 27 products are added together. That gives us one entry in the output, which in this simple example is a matrix. The Rubik's cube is the filter. It has one $3 \times 3$ grid of weights for each channel of the input volume, so it has three channels, whereas the filters in section \ref{convolutionalLayer} had just one. A filter applied to the stacked feature maps of figures \ref{cnn_filter} and \ref{cnn_filter2} would have two channels.\footnote{The word ``kernel'' is also used in this context. Sometimes it is a complete synonym for ``filter,'' and sometimes it means one two-dimensional grid of weights in a filter, the part applied to a single input channel. This chapter just uses ``filter.''}

Thus filters only move along the height and width dimensions.\footnote{This is not a necessary feature (filters with fewer channels than the input are possible and sometimes even useful), but it is pretty standard.} Figure \ref{filterChannels} makes the point with a variety of tensors. In each case, the receptive field of a filter at one stage of a convolution is shown ``inside'' the input volume, and as can be seen in each case, it spans all the channels of the input.

By equation \ref{eq:sliding_window}, the output width is $6 - 3 + 1 = 4$, and the same goes for the height. So the output is $1 \times 4 \times 4$, a four-by-four matrix.

\section{Filter Banks}\label{sect_filterBank}

% More on representational width. Link to transformers and multi-head attention

In general, a convolutional layer will involve multiple filters, a \glossary{filter bank}, each of which produces a separate feature map.\footnote{It is helpful to think of a bank of distinct filters, each of which learns a different way of processing the input, but in practice filter banks are stored as rank-4 tensors. For example, in PyTorch, a bank of 96 filters, each with shape $3 \times 11 \times 11$ would be stored as a single tensor with shape $(96, 3, 11, 11)$, where the dimensions represent (number of filters, input channels, height, width). The number of filters is also the number of output channels, so this shape is sometimes written $O \times I \times H \times W$ (output channels, input channels, filter height, filter width), which is how Simbrain labels it (see also section \extref{sect_tensors}). Simbrain displays a filter bank as a grid of small matrices. To picture it, think of the stack of Rubik's cubes in figure \ref{filterBank}, one per output channel, with each cube spread out from left to right, one slice per input channel. So each row of the grid is one filter, and each column is one input channel.} The two filters of figures \ref{cnn_filter} and \ref{cnn_filter2} already form a small filter bank: applied to the same input, they produce two feature maps, which together make a $2 \times 7 \times 7$ output volume. Figure \ref{filterBank} illustrates the idea. Each filter produces its own output, and the results are concatenated into a new output volume. That is, we just repeat the process discussed in figure \ref{filterToVolume} but do it separately for each filter. In this example, we get a bunch of matrix outputs and concatenate them into one output volume. That is why we said at the end of the last section that the number of channels in an output volume just corresponds to the number of filters we use.

In figure \ref{filterBank}, each filter has 3 channels because the input has 3 channels, and the output has 5 channels because there are 5 filters.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\accessibleimage[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/filterBank.pdf}{Filter-bank diagram, with one filter labeled: five filters, each 3 by 3 by 3, are applied to a 3 by 6 by 6 input volume with stride one, producing a 5 by 4 by 4 output volume whose number of channels equals the number of filters.}
\caption[Soraya Boza and Jeff Yoshimi.]{Result of applying a filter bank of 5 filters, each $3 \times 3 \times 3$, to a $3 \times 6 \times 6$ input volume with a stride of 1 to produce an output volume that is $5 \times 4 \times 4$. The $5$ output channels come from the $5$ filters in the bank; the output height and width come from equation \ref{eq:sliding_window}.}
\label{filterBank}
\end{figure}

To summarize, the output shape of a convolutional layer is:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
&\text{Output channels} &&= \text{number of filters}\\
&\text{Output height} &&= \text{input height} - \text{filter height} + 1\\
&\text{Output width} &&= \text{input width} - \text{filter width} + 1
\end{alignat*}
(For other strides and padding, use equation \ref{eq:sliding_window_general} for height and width.) For example, suppose an input volume has shape $3 \times 64 \times 64$, and we apply a filter bank with 32 filters, each of shape $3 \times 5 \times 5$. Then:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
&\text{Output channels} &&= 32\\
&\text{Output height} &&= 64 - 5 + 1 = 60\\
&\text{Output width} &&= 64 - 5 + 1 = 60
\end{alignat*}
So the output shape is $32 \times 60 \times 60$.

Through gradient descent (section \extref{sect_gradient_descent}), each filter in a bank comes to detect a different feature, so each feature map ends up representing the input in a different way. This generalizes the concept of the \glossary{representational width} of a layer (section \extref{structureNets}) to CNNs. Just as more nodes make a regular node layer of a feed-forward network more powerful, so too do more filters make a convolutional layer more powerful. Rather than learning just one way to represent inputs, a bank of filters can learn multiple complementary ways to represent inputs.

The representations are not programmed in but are learned via gradient descent. This is remarkable. We did not tell the network we want it to learn to respond to edges. All we focus on in training a network is inputs and outputs, using a labeled dataset. Training data for these networks might involve images paired with numbers. The network is given nothing else but these training examples: if you see this picture, it's a 2; this picture is an 8, etc. Then the network adjusts all its parameters (all the weights in its convolutional layers), in such a way as to reduce error.

\section{Multiple Convolutional Layers}

Now we stack these things on top of each other, add a new kind of layer, and also use some old-school neural network components. A deep network assembles these various pieces together.

The idea here is to combine \glossary{representational width} with \glossary{representational depth}.
Recall from the history chapter that the concept of layered feature detection goes back to Oliver Selfridge and his ``pandemonium'' model, according to which seeing letters involves a hierarchy of ``demons'' passing messages along: from edge demons to curve demons and finally to the output layer's ``B demon'' (see figure \extref{selfridge} in chapter \extref{ch_history}). This allows the network to learn to identify not just simple features like edges or curves, but also \emph{features of features}, like combinations of curves which make more complex shapes, and then combinations of these shapes. Deep networks build on this old idea, allowing us to train many-layered networks which develop highly sophisticated feature detectors.\footnote{They also allow these features to be visualized. The results can be quite strange and even disturbing. See \weburl{https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/} for some striking demonstrations.}

There are several kinds of volume-to-volume layer in a convolutional network. One is a filter bank, as discussed above. The other is a \glossary{pooling layer}, which reduces the amount of information passing through the network without altering its basic structure (section \ref{sect_pooling}). Finally, we can \glossary{flatten} a layer, which takes us from the convolutional layers back to a traditional feed-forward  node and weight layers (section \ref{sect_flatten}). Flattening a layer involves taking all the values in a tensor and simply arranging them into a single vector. A flattened $3 \times 3$ matrix, for example, would be a vector with 9 components. See figure \ref{deepNetExample}.

% From Yamins, 2016: "Deep networks through stacking. Since convolutional layer outputs have the same spatial layout as their inputs, output of one layer can be input to another. HCNNs can thus be stacked into deep networks (figure 1c). Although the local fields seen by units in a single layer have a fixed, small size, the effective receptive field size relative to the original input increases with succeeding layers. Because of repeated striding, deep HCNNs typically become less retinotopic with each succeeding layer, consistent with empirical observations4."

% Compositional or layered complexity. Example: the first layer of filters captures patterns like edges, corners, dots etc. Subsequent layers combine those patterns to make bigger patterns (like combining edges to make squares, circles, etc.). Now as we move forward in the layers, the patterns get more complex; hence there are larger combinations of patterns to capture. That's why we increase the filter size in subsequent layers to capture as many combinations as possible.

A \glossary{pooling layer} is useful when tensors start getting too big. The idea is to go from bigger to smaller tensors, to keep things more computationally manageable. But we must do it in a way that preserves important information. Here again we pass a window over the input and slide it left-to-right and top-to-bottom, but instead of taking a dot product, we apply one of two common pooling operations:

\begin{quote}
\begin{description}
\item[Max pooling] In each window, the maximum number is taken and written to an output matrix.
\item[Average pooling] The average value across a window is written to an output matrix.
\end{description}
\end{quote}
The result of this operation is an output that is smaller than the input. These are sometimes called subsampling or downsampling methods. They are valuable in that they reduce the size of the representations passing through the network while preserving important structures. Figure \ref{maxPooling} shows max pooling applied to the feature map from figure \ref{cnn_filter}. The pooled output is much smaller, but it still shows where the filter matched the image most strongly. Since each pooled value is the maximum over a window, a feature can shift slightly within its window without changing the output. This makes the network less sensitive to small shifts in where features appear.

% There is an issue that "feature map" is overloaded both for 3d volumes and for slices of 3d volumes
The pooling window has a \glossary{pool shape} consisting of height and width (e.g., $2 \times 2$ or $3 \times 3$), but no channel dimension. We pass the pooling window over every feature map (every channel) separately. Thus pooling operations only slide in the spatial dimensions (height and width), not in the channel dimension. This preserves the distinct representations learned by each filter in the previous layer. Each channel represents a different learned feature, and we want to downsample each feature map independently rather than combining information across channels. (A subsequent convolutional layer can learn to combine information across channels.)

Pooling windows usually move in non-overlapping steps. For example, a $2 \times 2$ window moves 2 pixels at a time (stride = 2), so the windows do not overlap, and the height and width are cut in half. In this usual case the output shape of a pooling layer is:
\begin{alignat*}{2}
&\text{Output channels} &&= \text{number of input channels}\\
&\text{Output height} &&= \text{input height} \div \text{pool window height}\\
&\text{Output width} &&= \text{input width} \div \text{pool window width}
\end{alignat*}
rounding down if the window does not divide evenly. In figure \ref{maxPooling}, $7 \div 2$ rounds down to 3, and the last row and column of the feature map are dropped. (For other strides, use equation \ref{eq:sliding_window_general} with the pooling window in place of the filter.) For example, suppose an input volume has shape $16 \times 60 \times 60$, and we apply max pooling with pool shape $2 \times 2$. Then the output has 16 channels, and its height and width are both $60 \div 2 = 30$. So the output shape is $16 \times 30 \times 30$.


\section{Flattening and Dense Layers}\label{sect_flatten}

Another thing we can do, usually in the final layers of a deep CNN, is to flatten a volume back into a vector. If we have a $5 \times 5$ matrix, for example, we can flatten it into an activation vector for a node layer with 25 nodes. If we have a $4 \times 5 \times 10$ volume, this becomes the activation vector for a node layer with 200 nodes.  Note that the length of the flattened vector is just the product of the shape values for the larger tensor.

In this way, we can convert whatever high-rank tensor we have into a vector, and thereby get back into the world of  traditional neural networks focused on in much of the book.  Then we just do things as we've done before.  We can couple our complex convolutional layers to a standard feed-forward network.  These ordinary weight layers are the dense layers described at the start of the chapter.

The overall idea is to start with layers that learn these complex features, then to compress these representations with subsampling, and finally to present the results to the final layers, which are the kinds of standard feed-forward networks discussed in earlier chapters (for example, chapter \extref{ch_supervised}).\footnote{The Simbrain simulation \simref{Vision $>$ CNN simple demo} trains a small network of this kind, with two convolutional layers, max pooling, flattening, and a dense output layer, to classify simple line patterns. A similar network is trained on handwritten digits in \simref{Backprop $>$ CNN MNIST}.}

\section{Applications of Convolutional Networks}\label{cnn_applications}

Convolutional neural networks have applications in both engineering and cognitive science (recall the taxonomy of chapter \extref{ch_applications}).

In terms of engineering, as noted above, CNNs are associated with improvements in many areas, including image recognition, speech recognition and language translation \cite{lecun2015deep, goodfellow2016deep}. Historically, CNNs originate in computational neuroscience models of vision that were developed in the early 1980s \cite{fukushima1982neocognitron}. These ideas were later used to engineer pattern recognition networks. A famous early application was recognizing zip codes written on envelopes \cite{lecun1989backpropagation}. Since convolutional networks became mainstream based on technical improvements (big data, GPU and hardware acceleration, better architectures and training algorithms; see section \extref{deep_revolution}), they have become powerful tools in both applied and scientific contexts.

CNNs are also relevant to neuroscience and psychology because they develop internal representations that are remarkably similar to those in the human visual system. CNNs for vision often develop edge detectors in early layers, which are learned by training rather than being programmed in. This makes them valuable models of the visual cortex and examples of computational neuroscience. The emergence of these representations through training also illustrates an old connectionist theme: psychologically meaningful categories can emerge from training without being explicitly programmed. In NETtalk, phonetic categories like consonant and vowel emerged with training. With Elman's simple recurrent networks, grammatical categories like verb and noun emerged with training. Similarly, CNNs develop visual feature detectors through training alone. All three of these examples are discussed in more detail in chapter \extref{ch_representations}.

A particularly significant application of CNNs in cognitive science is illustrated by the pioneering work of Yamins and DiCarlo \cite{yamins2016using}, who used CNNs to study how the brain decodes sensory information through a cascade of simple neural operations. They trained a hierarchical convolutional neural network to recognize various objects, and then tested whether the resulting network could predict neural responses in the visual cortex. The results were impressive: the CNN's layers achieved the best predictions of neural activity in terms of explained variance, outperforming models specifically designed for this purpose. Moreover, the layers of such networks line up with stages of the visual pathway \cite{gucclu2015deep}: early layers, which develop edge detectors, best predict early visual areas like V1; intermediate layers best predict intermediate areas like V4; and top layers best predict the inferior temporal cortex (IT), which is involved in object recognition. This work is discussed in greater detail, with figures showing the network architecture and neural response comparisons, in chapter \extref{ch_representations} (see section \extref{deepVisionNets}). Also see the discussion of the visual system in chapter \extref{ch_neuro}.

One feature of CNNs has no direct counterpart in the brain: weight sharing (section \ref{convolutionalLayer}). There is no literal analog in the brain of sliding a filter over an input. Still, the visual system achieves something similar. Early visual areas contain detectors for the same simple features, such as edges at particular orientations, repeated across the whole visual field, an arrangement that inspired Fukushima's neocognitron \cite{fukushima1982neocognitron}. How the brain builds this repeated structure without shared weights is an open question. One possibility is that development produces similar circuits throughout a region of cortex. Another is that learning produces similar detectors at each location, because each part of the visual field is exposed to similar image statistics.
